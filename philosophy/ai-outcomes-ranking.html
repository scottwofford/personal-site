Philosophy Ranking AI Outcomes Wrong on Internet

Ranking AI Outcomes by Benefit to Humanity

Source: MATS Autumn 2026 application (application, Notion version). WIP and subject to change.

My North Star

I rank in two stages, and the order matters. Doing this honestly forced me to separate two things I'd been smushing together: what I personally value, and what actually deserves the top of the ranking. They aren't the same list, and the gap between them is most of what's interesting here.

Stage 1, the one-way-door filter. Some outcomes are about staying out of states we can't get back out of. In priority order:

  1. Don't go extinct as a species: the ultimate one-way door.
  2. Don't descend into chaos / anarchy / authoritarianism (i.e., don't reduce quality of life significantly).
  3. Increase human well-being: health, wealth, agency/autonomy.

A two-way door (we tried it, it was bad, we walked it back) is cheap to get wrong. A one-way door is not. So anything that reduces irreversible catastrophe sits in the top tier before any other consideration gets a vote. No amount of ordinary welfare buys its way past an extinction-magnitude risk.

Stage 2, the welfare tiebreaker. Among the outcomes that don't move the needle on a one-way door, I rank by who they actually reach, using two people to keep myself honest:

  1. My daughters' life outlook (of course I'm biased towards my kids ha).
  2. A low or middle-income child's life outlook: does this reach a kid born without much of a safety net, or only help people who were already fine?

The personas decide within Stage 2. They don't get to overturn Stage 1. An earlier draft of this let the personas look like they were doing the work while extinction-magnitude reasoning quietly did the real sorting underneath. Here they fire where they actually belong: below the irreversibility filter.

One caveat up front: the catastrophe odds people throw around (a 1 in 6 here, a 1 in 10 there) are point estimates I don't trust to two significant figures, even from people I respect. I lean on Toby Ord's framing as one anchor, not as a measurement.

The Ranking

Tier 1: one-way doors.

1. Building oversight mechanisms that detect when AI systems pursue unintended goals. This is the whole game for me, and it's literally what I work on at Luthien. I have personally spent 30+ hours per week with Claude or ChatGPT or Gemini since May 2024, outside of my job at Amazon. Based on that lived experience, AI systems today already often pursue unintended goals, the kind of prosaic misalignment Luthien is built to catch. With the scaling laws and the economic incentives to deploy AI across the economy, it seems very easy to imagine these unintended, costly, consequential mistakes becoming more common (even if AIs don't have their own "goals" per se). Over 5 or even 50 years, the risk without robust real-time oversight looks high and likely, and the strength of our current oversight relative to the speed at which capabilities are advancing is humblingly terrible. If we deploy systems we can't reliably oversee, every other item on this list is downstream of getting this wrong. Ord puts unaligned AI at about 1 in 10, his highest single existential estimate; I treat it as one anchor in a range I can't pin down, not a measurement.

2. Building defenses against AI-enabled biological threats. Don't go extinct as a species via an AI-enabled or worsened pandemic, which isn't at all hard to imagine given humanity's incompetence dealing with COVID. An engineered pathogen is the most concrete near-extinction pathway I can actually picture, and it's near-consensus top-tier among people who think about this for a living. Defense (detection, countermeasures, hardening) is asymmetric in the good direction: it lowers the worst case without itself opening a worse door. In June 2026, Dario Amodei, Sam Altman, and Demis Hassabis took the rare step of co-signing a letter to Congress warning that AI is eroding "the knowledge barriers which have historically prevented bad actors from obtaining biological weapons"; three competitors agreeing on one risk is about as strong a signal as this field produces. Worth noting their letter argues for misuse prevention (screening synthesis orders, gating model queries), so a defense-first framing goes a step beyond it.

3. Minimizing unrestricted access to state-of-the-art AI chips. This is the one I moved up, and I want to be precise about why, because an earlier draft got it backwards. Separate building the option to pause from using it. Building the option is good precisely because the option takes time to build, and we could find ourselves sliding toward catastrophic loss of control and be sad we didn't have it. That makes it irreversibility insurance, which is Tier 1. Using the option is a different thing, and it carries a very high cost: second-order effects, concentration of power, and yes, my own libertarian bias against control regimes. But the cost of pulling the lever is not a reason to refuse to build it. My earlier draft conflated the two and let my prior against pulling the trigger talk me out of owning the gun. The compute-governance camp (GovAI's case that compute is the most governable input, Karnofsky's if-then commitments, and Amodei on locking down the supply chain) treats this as a main concrete lever on the loss-of-control risk I rank at the top, and on reflection I think they're right.

Tier 2: large welfare, not extinction-magnitude.

4. Accelerating AI applications in scientific research. This is the cleanest example of what I value not being what I rank highest. I'm a huge proponent of accelerating AI in science, and getting closer to epistemic truth seems about 99% good with less risk. But I have to split two things I'd blurred. Truth-seeking itself looks close to all-upside. AI-for-science capability is dual-use: the same tools that design a cancer drug are also the fastest path to the engineered pathogen I rank at #2. So the thing I'm personally most excited about lands in Tier 2, because its enormous upside is welfare-shaped, not extinction-shaped, and its tail risk is already counted under bio defense. Loving something isn't a reason to misrank it.

5. Ensuring fair compensation for workers displaced by AI automation. This scores highest on my low and middle-income-child test, which is why it leads the welfare tier. I'm more optimistic on economic paradigms where creativity and agency are the dominating source of purpose compared to UBI-like systems. I do think a basic safety net of helping people feel safe and meet basic needs is a great investment, given that about 10.3% of the population makes less than $3/day. The literature on well-being up to a certain point of income is robust, but I think it's hard to measure who was "displaced," so better to use needs-based economic redistribution vs. social justice-based economic redistribution. It's not a one-way door for the species, but it's close to one for individual lives, which is why it sits at the top of Tier 2.

6. Detecting and labeling AI-generated misinformation and synthetic media. I demoted this, and I want to be honest about why, because it's the thing I say I'm personally most worried about: scenarios where AI amplifies misinformation (e.g., via sycophancy), or just creates more chaos due to system-design choices or some combination of human tendencies (e.g., fickle, vain) and the long tail of prosaic misalignment. So why isn't it higher? An earlier draft double-counted. I'd quietly imported a pandemic's death toll (AI-amplified misinformation during a bioengineered outbreak) when that catastrophe already sits at #2, and I'd backed the rank with a multiplier I basically made up. Strip out the borrowed catastrophe and what's left is an information-ecosystem and trust problem: corrosive, civilizationally serious, reaches both of my personas, but reversible at the scale of any single failure. It's the welfare-tier item whose mechanism I fear most, but counted honestly it isn't extinction-shaped, so it doesn't crack Tier 1.

7. Building AI-powered robotic systems that can learn from physical interaction with their environment. Broadly useful, with the main downsides being military applications, and it seems like the cat's already out of the bag on that with drones, so the marginal contribution of this relative to the upside seems favorable. Big diffuse welfare upside (labor, eldercare, the physical work humans can't or won't do), mostly two-way-door, reaches both personas eventually.

8. Protecting creators' work from unauthorized use in AI training data. Only ~2% to ~6% of humans, and it's not clear how severely this negatively affects their well-being. It matters for fairness and for keeping a functioning creative economy that my daughters will live inside of, but it's a rights-and-redistribution question, fully reversible, and narrower in reach than the items above it.

Tier 3: real, but second-order.

9. Reducing the environmental impact of AI data centers. Genuinely good, and I dug into the energy scenarios enough to take it seriously (appendix below). But it's second-order: it makes scaling cleaner rather than directly changing whether the future goes well or badly. Climate change is important, but not existential. In Ord's framing, climate is about 1 in 1,000 this century versus unaligned AI at about 1 in 10, and he thinks climate risk has fallen since 2020. Data centers were about 415 TWh in 2024, roughly 1.5% of global electricity.

Frontier training power is doubling roughly every year; single runs are projected to draw 4 to 16 GW by 2030. What matters here isn't raw energy, it's emissions and local effects. Even at 10x, data centers stay a small single-digit share of global emissions; the bigger lever is regional grid stress, electricity-price spikes, and water use.

10. Reducing the computational cost of training frontier AI models. Let me steelman the bottom item, because it isn't nothing: cheaper compute democratizes access, which my low-income persona likes, and accelerates the science I value at #4. I also think the market is taking care of it. Worth separating two things: cost per unit of capability is falling fast (algorithmic and hardware efficiency), while absolute frontier training cost is rising about 2.4x a year. So the market takes care of efficiency, not total spend or energy. But it's the purest accelerant on the list: it speeds up everything indiscriminately, including the capabilities I'm most worried about, and does nothing on its own to make the destination safer. An accelerant with no steering goes last. (This sits in tension with #9: cheaper compute means more compute means more energy demand. I'm ranking the cost-reduction outcome itself as least beneficial, and treating the environmental cleanup as the separate, slightly-more-beneficial mitigation. They're not the same item, and I don't want to pretend the energy logic is clean.)

What would change my mind

  • Chips (#3): if compute governance turned out to be unbuildable without an enforcement regime so centralizing that it creates its own catastrophic lock-in, the insurance would cost more than the risk it hedges, and it drops out of Tier 1.
  • Oversight (#1): if scalable oversight turned out to be roughly solved by default as capabilities rise (alignment-by-default), my #1 collapses into a Tier 2 nice-to-have.
  • Misinformation (#6): if someone showed me a credible mechanism by which an information-ecosystem failure becomes genuinely irreversible at civilizational scale (without borrowing the pandemic's weight), I'd promote it back to Tier 1.
  • Science (#4): if the dual-use tail of AI-for-science turned out to be sharply containable, the thing I love would also be the thing I rank highest, and I'd be happy about it.

Where I diverge from the field

Roughly: on bio defense and oversight I'm with the near-consensus top. On chips I've moved toward the compute-governance camp (GovAI, Karnofsky, and Amodei on the lab side) and away from the accelerationist view that ranks it near the bottom. One nuance I got wrong at first and want to be honest about: the technical alignment researchers I most admire (Christiano, Shlegeris, Russell, Bengio) actually put their effort into oversight, not compute policy, so ranking oversight above chips tracks where they spend their time, not just my own libertarian discount. My one real divergence is misinformation: I rank it higher within the welfare tier than most of the field does, because the sycophancy-and-chaos mechanism is the one I find most threatening, even after I make myself admit it isn't extinction-shaped. On science I sit between the camps: lab leaders and accelerationists put it at the top, the safety crowd puts it mid, and so do I, for the specific reason that I won't let how much I love it override where the dual-use risk says it belongs.
